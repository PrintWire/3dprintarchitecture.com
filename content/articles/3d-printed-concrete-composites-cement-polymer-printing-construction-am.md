Cement-based materials can carry large loads, but they are brittle: once a crack starts, it can spread suddenly. **3D printed concrete composites** developed at Princeton University take a different route from conventional reinforcement. A multi-material printer places thin polymer layers inside the printed mortar, and those soft layers interrupt cracks as they try to cross the part.

The headline results come from mortar printed with thin polyurethane interlayers: fracture toughness up to 187-fold higher than monolithic cast mortar and ductility up to 22.6-fold higher than the monolithic references, with flexural strength statistically comparable to the monolithic references.

---

## The Core Idea: Borrowing Architecture from Nature

The design is modelled on *Euplectella aspergillum*, a deep-sea glass sponge whose silica skeleton is built from alternating hard and soft layers. The Princeton team, led by Reza Moini, assistant professor of civil and environmental engineering, with associate research scholar Aimane Najmeddine as lead author, reproduced that layered hard-soft arrangement in a cement-based material. Princeton describes the result as a new class of material, Architected Cementitious Composites.

The polymer layers do not work like rebar. According to the paper, they arrest and deflect cracks, bridge them, and force a crack to form again in each following mortar layer instead of running straight through.

---

## What the Numbers Mean in Practice

### The baselines matter

The published figures are comparisons against specific references, and they differ depending on the reference:

- With silicone interlayers, the first version of the composite showed up to 3.9-fold higher fracture toughness than monolithic 3D printed mortar and 8.8-fold higher than cast mortar. Ductility was up to 11.7-fold and 12.4-fold higher, respectively.
- With thin, stiffer polyurethane interlayers, fracture toughness was 82-fold higher than the 3D printed monolithic reference and 187-fold higher than the cast reference, and ductility was 22.6-fold higher.

The 187-fold figure is therefore the best case, measured against cast monolithic mortar.

### Design drives performance

The step from silicone to polyurethane was guided by simulation. The team's modelling indicated that stiffer, thinner soft layers could raise the work of fracture by up to 24 times while keeping the load-bearing capacity of monolithic mortar. The mortar-polyurethane composites were built on that finding, and their flexural strength came out statistically comparable to monolithic mortar.

---

## Cement Polymer Printing: How It Works

The team used a custom multi-material 3D printer. A computer-guided system alternates extrusion between mortar and polymer, so thin elastomer layers are placed within the printed part as it is built.

Princeton says the approach could be applied to existing gantry or robotic concrete printing systems by adding a secondary print head that co-prints polymer with concrete. The researchers also say scaling up still needs work.

---

## Implications for Construction Practice

The researchers point to structural walls, facades and protective elements as possible uses, along with components exposed to impact, vibration, wind, waves or seismic loads. They also suggest that printed walls could combine concrete boundaries with a co-printed insulating polymer.

None of this has been demonstrated at building scale. The published results are material-level measurements.

---

## What Comes Next

According to Princeton, future work includes scaling the approach and testing how the composites perform under full structural and environmental conditions.
